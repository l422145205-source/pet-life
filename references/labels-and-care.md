# Labels and care: the distinctions that matter

Reviewed against primary sources on 2026-10-29. These support extraction and the Skill's scope, not product endorsements or veterinary certification.

[AAFCO — Reading Labels](https://aafco.org/consumers/understanding-pet-food/reading-labels/) explains calorie statements using kcal per kg as-fed and familiar units such as cans or cups. Feeding directions may need adjustment for a particular animal. A nutritional adequacy statement, species and life stage are separate label facts; do not infer them from a product name or ingredient list.

Preserve the source unit before conversion. A product at 3800 kcal/kg has 3.8 kcal/g. A product at 80 kcal/can needs an explicit can mass before comparison by grams. Equal price per calorie does not establish equal nutritional suitability. These examples describe arithmetic, not a diet recommendation.

[WSAVA — Global Nutrition Guidelines](https://wsava.org/global-guidelines/global-nutrition-guidelines/) treats nutritional assessment as individualized and provides diet-history tools for owners and veterinary teams. Pet characteristics, food and feeding management all matter.

Record existing daily quantities and their source. Use them for inventory and packing. Do not calculate a new calorie target from body weight alone, rank foods as medically suitable, or change a therapeutic diet. For health questions, organize observations for a veterinarian; bookkeeping can still be completed.

On sitter cards keep each pet's quantities, times, location and separation instructions distinct. Copy only explicit handling instructions. Missing emergency contacts/arrangements mean the card is a draft; do not invent a nearby clinic or an emergency threshold. Do not invent medication schedules or turn a reminder into a completed treatment record.
